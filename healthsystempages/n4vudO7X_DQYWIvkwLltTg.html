Jun 16, 2025
Brian FitzGerald of South Carolina experienced tingling hands and malfunctioning fingers in the summer of 2024, leading him to seek medical help. After seeing neurologists and orthopedists, a brain scan revealed a tumor, which was determined to be melanoma that had spread to his brain. Seeking a second opinion, FitzGerald came to the Duke Center for Brain and Spine Metastasis (DCBSM). The DCBSM team, including New Patient Coordinator Eris Worlds and nurse navigators Donie Magee and Scott Pollard, rapidly coordinated his care, streamlining the transfer of medical information and scheduling appointments with the multidisciplinary team. Peter Fecci, MD, PhD, performed surgery, and FitzGerald subsequently received radiation treatment from Zach Reitman, MD, PhD, and immunotherapy from April Salama, MD. Following treatment, FitzGerald reported feeling "like a new person," with clear scans and no pain, expressing significant gratitude for the care he received at Duke.
Jun 16, 2025
Duke University School of Medicine researchers have developed two new frameworks for evaluating the performance, safety, and reliability of large-language models (LLMs) in healthcare. Published in npj Digital Medicine and the Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association (JAMIA), these studies aim to provide a rigorous and scalable approach for health systems to assess AI systems used in clinical settings. As LLMs are increasingly integrated into medical practice for tasks such as generating clinical notes and summarizing patient communications, there is a growing need for robust evaluation methods. The SCRIBE framework, detailed in the npj Digital Medicine study, is designed for ambient digital scribing tools and utilizes expert clinical reviews, automated scoring, and simulated testing to evaluate accuracy, fairness, coherence, and resilience. The related study in JAMIA applies a complementary framework to assess LLMs used within the Epic platform for drafting patient message replies, comparing clinician feedback with automated metrics. This research highlights the importance of continuous evaluation to ensure AI tools support care delivery effectively and safely.
Jun 16, 2025
Internal Medicine Residents at Duke Regional Hospital are implementing new performance metrics to improve patient care and better understand how their daily tasks influence healthcare system outcomes. This initiative by Drs. Akhila Belur, John Hoyle, Komal Safdar, and Pahresah Roomiany involves structured weekly huddles at Duke Regional Hospital during the general medicine rotation. The goal is to educate residents on Systems-Based Practice (SBP), a core competency recognized by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education. These metrics include length of stay, readmission rates, case mix index (CMI), and discharge documentation accuracy, which are critical for evaluating hospital performance, securing reimbursement, and improving patient outcomes. By increasing residents' awareness of these metrics, the program aims to bridge the gap between medical training and the practical impact of their work on the healthcare system and patient care.

Mary Klotman, MD

Executive Vice President for Health Affairs, Duke University, Dean, Duke University School of Medicine, and Chief Academic Officer, Duke Health

Mary E. Klotman, MD, is a nationally recognized leader in academic medicine. She currently serves as the Executive Vice President for Health Affairs at Duke University, Dean of the Duke University School of Medicine, and Chief Academic Officer for Duke Health. Dr. Klotman earned both her undergraduate degree in Zoology and her medical degree from Duke University. She completed her internal medicine residency and a fellowship in Infectious Diseases at Duke before becoming an assistant professor of medicine there. She joined the National Institutes of Health (NIH) in 1991, where she researched HIV under the direction of Dr. Robert C. Gallo. In 1994, she moved to the Mount Sinai School of Medicine, serving as the Irene and Dr. Arthur M. Fishberg Professor of Medicine and Chief of the Division of Infectious Diseases for 13 years. She also co-directed Mount Sinai's Global Health and Emerging Pathogens Institute. Dr. Klotman returned to Duke in 2010 as Professor and Chair of the Department of Medicine, becoming the first woman to hold that position. She was appointed Dean of the Duke University School of Medicine and Vice Chancellor for Health Affairs in 2017. In June 2023, she was appointed Duke's first Executive Vice President for Health Affairs, assuming the role in July 2023. She also became the Chief Academic Officer for Duke Health in 2020. Her research has primarily focused on the molecular pathogenesis of HIV-1 infection, including demonstrating that HIV resides and evolves separately in kidney cells. Dr. Klotman is an elected member of the National Academy of Medicine and the American Academy of Arts and Sciences. She is a past president of the Association of American Physicians and the Association of Professors of Medicine. She also served as editor of the Annual Review of Medicine.
